Cast Iron Care: How to Season, Wash, and Restore Cast Iron Pans

By Marcus Thorne • 8 Min Read

A cast iron skillet is a legendary kitchen workhorse. It can transition seamlessly from searing a steak on a hot stovetop to baking cornbread in a hot oven. With proper care, a cast iron pan lasts for generations, developing a natural, non-stick cooking surface. However, many home cooks avoid cast iron because they think it is difficult to maintain or clean. The rules of cast iron care—seasoning, washing, and storing—are simple when you understand the basic science of oil polymerization. This guide reviews the chemistry, washing methods, and rust restoration steps to help you care for your cast iron cookware.

1. The Science of Seasoning: Oil Polymerization

The dark, shiny finish on a cast iron pan is not paint; it is **seasoning**. Seasoning is formed through a chemical process called **polymerization**, where thin layers of oil are baked onto the iron surface at high temperatures. The intense heat causes the liquid fat molecules to bond, creating a hard, plastic-like protective layer that fills the microscopic pores of the iron, preventing rust and creating a natural non-stick surface.

To season a pan:

  • Choose the Right Oil: Use an oil with high unsaturated fats and a high smoke point, such as flaxseed, grapeseed, or canola oil.
  • Apply a Micro-Layer: Rub a teaspoon of oil across the clean pan, then use a clean paper towel to wipe *almost all* of it off. The layer must be extremely thin to prevent a sticky finish.
  • Bake at High Heat: Place the pan upside down in an oven preheated to 450°F (230°C) for one hour, allowing the oil to polymerize completely. Repeat 2 to 3 times to build a durable base.
"Cast iron seasoning is chemistry. Baking thin layers of unsaturated oils onto the iron creates a natural non-stick surface that lasts for generations."

2. Washing Guidelines: Soap is Not the Enemy

A common myth is that you should never use dish soap on cast iron. This rule dates back to historical soaps made with harsh lye, which could strip the seasoning. Modern liquid dish soaps are gentle and will not damage polymerized oil layers. Follow these washing steps:

  • Wash Gently: Use warm water, a mild dish soap, and a non-abrasive sponge to clean food residues. Avoid harsh steel wool scrubbers, which can scratch the seasoning.
  • Use Salt for Stuck Food: For stubborn, stuck-on food, pour coarse kosher salt into the dry pan and scrub with a dry paper towel. The salt acts as a gentle abrasive, cleaning the pan without scratching the seasoning.
  • Dry Immediately: Water is the enemy of raw iron. Dry the pan completely with a towel, then place it on a warm stovetop burner for 2 minutes to evaporate any remaining moisture.

3. Preventing Rust and Storing Safely

After drying the warm pan, apply a tiny drop of oil (grapeseed or canola) to the interior surface and rub it in with a paper towel. This creates a protective barrier against moisture in the air. Store your pans in a dry cupboard. If you stack your cast iron skillets, place a paper towel between each pan to prevent scratches.

4. How to Restore a Rusted Pan

If your pan gets wet and rusts, do not throw it away. Cast iron is durable and can be restored. Scrub the rust away using warm soapy water and a steel scouring pad to expose the raw grey iron. Wash and dry the pan completely, then follow the seasoning steps to build a fresh, protective oil coat.
